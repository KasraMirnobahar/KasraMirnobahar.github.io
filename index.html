Why do we keep checking our phones, even when nothing is there?

I'm Kasra Mirnobahar. I study how social media, reward and decision-making shape behaviour and mental health, using experiments, computational models, real-world data and brain imaging.

This page is the story of how I got to that question, and where I would like to take it next.

i.2019 – 2023Tehran

Starting with people, and with what goes wrong

I studied psychology at the University of Social Welfare and Rehabilitation Sciences in Tehran and finished with first-class honours. Most of my time there was spent close to clinical work. For my dissertation I designed a study with 600 adults on childhood poly-victimisation, which means being exposed to several kinds of harm while growing up, and how it relates to impulsivity and behaviour problems later in life. That work became my first two papers.

Alongside it I ran cognitive tasks with five-to-eight-year-olds in schools, and for a year I volunteered as a counsellor on a suicide crisis line. Those years gave me a feel for running studies from ethics to analysis. They also showed me that the numbers in a mental health dataset are people.

BSc Psychology, USWR, first class (17.58/20) Two papers, 2025: Iran J Psychiatry Behav Sci and Int J High Risk Behav Addict
ii.2023Tehran

The question shows up

In my last year I worked as a research assistant on a project about problematic social media use. We recruited clinical and non-clinical samples, and I helped collect and clean the data and write it up. A paper from that line of work, on fear of missing out, belonging and social isolation, is now under review at Scientific Reports.

What stayed with me was how blunt the tools were. Most studies asked how many hours people spent on their phones. Hours don't explain the urge to pick the phone up again a minute after putting it down.

iii.2024 – 2025Glasgow

Building a social media feed in the lab

I came to Glasgow on a Behavioural Sciences Scholarship for an MSc in Behavioural Sciences. For my dissertation, supervised by Federica Farolfi and Georgia Turner, I wanted to test an old idea from learning theory: that rewards arriving at unpredictable times keep us coming back.

So I built a simulated social media feed in Gorilla. People could scroll, post their own content and receive likes. I then varied whether the likes arrived at predictable or unpredictable times. I recruited 110 people and analysed the data in R.

When the rewards were unpredictable, people checked more.

I presented this at the Connected Minds Lab in Amsterdam and at the BPS Cyberpsychology conference, and I am writing it up as The Social Media Skinner Box.

Read the full project, with analysis and code MSc Behavioural Sciences, University of Glasgow, Merit
iv.2025 – 2026Glasgow

Looking at the brain while it decides

Behaviour is half the picture, so I joined the Philiastides Lab as a research assistant. With Dr Gina Brunner I collected simultaneous EEG and fMRI from 45 participants on perceptual decisions and how confident people feel about them, and preprocessed the EEG in MATLAB and EEGLAB. With Dr Hamed Ghane I ran 30 EEG and ECG sessions on how the heartbeat affects learning and choice, and cleaned and analysed that data in Python.

v.2026 – nowGlasgow, Cambridge, Amsterdam

Out of the lab, into real lives

Lab tasks can only go so far, so my current work uses real-world data. With David Grüning, Georgia Turner, Wouter van den Bos, Myranda.ai and How We Feel (Max Planck Institute, Cambridge and Amsterdam), I am combining people's actual social media data with short daily mood surveys to see how emotion and scrolling feed into each other.

With Dr Samuel Leighton and Rajeev Krishnadas I am working on NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde health records from people with psychosis. We are writing a target trial emulation protocol, a way of asking causal questions of routine data, and using it on related questions about antipsychotic medication and weight gain. It is a different topic, and it gives me the causal methods I want for social media research.

vi.nextPhD

One person at a time

Social media research keeps giving mixed answers, partly because it treats billions of users as one group. My proposed PhD project, person-specific digital phenotyping of social media use and digital well-being, brings the threads above together. It combines donated social media data, experience sampling and computational measures of behaviour to model how each person's own use and mood move together over time.

The goal is interventions and policy that fit the person, not an average user who doesn't exist. I'll present the proposal at the Digital Mental Health Conference in Cambridge in December 2026, and I am looking for a PhD position to do it.
